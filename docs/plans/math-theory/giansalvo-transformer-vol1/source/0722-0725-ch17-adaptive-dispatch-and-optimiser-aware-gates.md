### 17.7.4 Optimiser-induced residual drift

The slow-drift condition becomes operational once the change in $A_{\rm res}$ is related to the optimisation step. Let the residual operator be viewed locally as a differentiable function of the trainable parameters, $A_{\rm res}=A_{\rm res}(\theta)$. A generic first-order optimisation step can be written as

<a id="display-0722-generic-preconditioned-step"></a>
$$
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta P_tg_t,
$$

where $g_t$ is the gradient and $P_t$ is the optimiser-dependent preconditioner. For SGD, $P_t=I$; for momentum or Adam, $P_t$ encodes the corresponding moment-dependent scaling.

<a id="proposition-17-19"></a>
**Proposition 17.19 (Optimiser-induced residual drift).** *Assume that $A_{\rm res}(\theta)$ is differentiable and that one optimisation step has the form*

<a id="display-0722-optimiser-drift-step-hypothesis"></a>
$$
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta P_tg_t.
$$

*Then*

<a id="display-0722-optimiser-induced-residual-drift"></a>
$$
\|A_{\rm res}(\theta_{t+1})-A_{\rm res}(\theta_t)\|
\le\eta\|D_\theta A_{\rm res}(\theta_t)\|\,\|P_tg_t\|+O\bigl(\eta^2\|P_tg_t\|^2\bigr).
$$

*Consequently, the spectral gate remains in the slow-drift regime when*

<a id="display-0722-optimiser-slow-drift-criterion"></a>
$$
\eta\|D_\theta A_{\rm res}(\theta_t)\|\,\|P_tg_t\|\ll\delta_{\rm out}(t).
$$

*Proof sketch.* A first-order Taylor expansion of $A_{\rm res}(\theta)$ at $\theta_t$ with increment $-\eta P_tg_t$ gives the stated bound. Substitution into Lemma 17.18 yields the slow-drift condition. $\square$

The proposition clarifies the interface with the Adam and preconditioning analysis of Chapter 16. The dispatch mechanism is not optimiser-invariant: it is stable when the optimiser-induced spectral movement is small compared with the external gap, and it should be delayed or smoothed when this inequality is violated.

### 17.7.5 First-order Lie closure of dNTK perturbations

The Lie-algebraic extension of the dNTK theory is deeper than the numerical questions above. A complete theorem would require closure not only of the first variation of the network map but also of its curvature terms. A safe partial statement is nevertheless possible at first order.

<a id="proposition-17-20"></a>
**Proposition 17.20 (First-order closure of Lie-constrained dNTK dynamics).** *Let the admissible parameter perturbations be constrained to a Lie subalgebra $\mathfrak g$. Assume that the network differential $D_\theta f_\theta$ maps $\mathfrak g$ into a closed feature subspace $\mathcal H_{\mathfrak g}$, and that the induced tangent kernel is built only from inner products in $\mathcal H_{\mathfrak g}$. Then the first-order variation of the tangent kernel remains in the kernel tangent cone generated by $\mathfrak g$.*

The unresolved point is higher-order closure. Even when the first-order variation is Lie-compatible, the dynamic NTK may leave the Lie-generated kernel family through second-order curvature terms unless $D_\theta^2f_\theta[\xi,\zeta]$ is compatible with the same feature subspace for $\xi,\zeta\in\mathfrak g$. Thus the general Lie-subalgebraic dNTK theorem remains a genuine open problem, while the first-order mechanism already explains why Centered Attention in Chapter 15 behaves as a closed antisymmetric motor.

<a id="section-17-7-6"></a>
### 17.7.6 Resulting dependency chain

The partial resolutions above change the status of the operational open questions. The numerical programme is no longer a list of independent unknowns, but a dependency chain:

<a id="display-0723-optimiser-aware-dependency-chain"></a>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat\varepsilon_{\rm floor}&\Longrightarrow\gamma_{\rm near}(t)\Longrightarrow\text{stable dispatch}\\
&\Longrightarrow\text{drifting-subspace tracking}\Longrightarrow\text{optimiser-aware gating}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The remaining open work is therefore sharper: the constants in Proposition 17.16 should be made non-asymptotic; the adaptive threshold of Definition 17.17 should be validated in the real-scale experiments of Chapter 18; and the first-order Lie closure of Proposition 17.20 should be extended, or shown not to extend, to the full dynamic NTK.

## 17.8 Concluding Remarks

The unified gate of §17.5 is a strict refinement of the bidirectional gate of Chapter 15, and the empirical verifications of §§17.4.2, 17.4.4, and 17.5.2 confirm the asymptotic predictions of the underlying lemmas. The framework nonetheless rests on three structural assumptions whose relaxation defines genuine open problems, and leaves five operational questions partially answered. Both are catalogued for posterity. The dNTK Lie-closure problem is not listed separately below, because Section 17.7.5 has already recast it as a projected-closure problem controlled by the measurable leakage functional $\mathcal L_{\mathfrak g}(t)$.

<a id="section-17-8-1"></a>
### 17.8.1 Structural limitations

**Limitation 1: locality of Rayleigh quotient iteration.** Lemma 17.8 guarantees cubic convergence locally, in a neighbourhood of the target eigenvector. Outside this neighbourhood, RQI may oscillate or converge to a non-target eigenvector, and the iteration is not even guaranteed to be a contraction. The unified gate addresses this by preceding RQI with a Lanczos scoping phase (§17.4.3), which provides a Ritz vector that is in the basin of attraction of the target. The implicit assumption is that the Lanczos starting point is *always* within the basin of attraction of the same eigenvector that RQI then refines. This is plausible by the Kaniel-Paige-Saad bound (Lemma 17.9), but the precise geometric statement — “the Ritz vector returned by Lanczos $m=2$ lies entirely within the basin of attraction of $v_1(A)$ for any input vector with non-negligible overlap with $v_1$” — is not formally established in the chapter. A quantitative bound on the basin radius as a function of the spectrum of $A$ would close this gap; it is not provided here.

**Limitation 2: assumption of bounded second moment.** Theorem 17.11 (and its parent, Theorem 16.11 of Chapter 16) requires bounded second moment of the input distribution, $\mathbb E[\|X\|_F^2]<\infty$. This is satisfied by all standard Transformer training distributions, but the assumption is not innocuous: under heavy-tailed input statistics — which arise in some adversarial or out-of-distribution settings — the Robbins-Monro framework underlying the gate convergence may fail. The experimental verifications were performed on bounded

synthetic data; the theory does not extend trivially to heavy-tailed inputs, and the empirical behaviour in such regimes is unstudied.

**Limitation 3: floating-point floor of the iteration.** The empirical verification of Lemma 17.10 in Figure 17.6 reveals a floor of $\sim8\cdot10^{-7}$ in the bias of regularised Block MCA EXIN, below which the Lemma’s linear scaling no longer applies. The floor is the residual error of the Stiefel-flow discretisation itself, dependent on learning rate $\eta$, iteration count $T$, embedding dimension $d$, and block size $m$. No analytic expression is provided for this floor as a function of these parameters; its existence was discovered empirically and forced a revision of the operational recommendation for $\varepsilon_{\rm reg}$. A discretisation analysis in the spirit of (?, Chapter 4) would yield a floor of the form $f(\eta,T,d,m)\cdot\|A_{\rm res}\|$; this is left as future work.

### 17.8.2 Open questions

**Open question 1: adaptive online estimation of the iteration floor.** The revised recommendation of §17.5.2 prescribes choosing $\varepsilon_{\rm reg}$ “one decade above the empirically estimated iteration floor”, but does not specify how to estimate the floor online during INCRT training. A simple heuristic is to run a warmup pass of regularised Block MCA EXIN with $\varepsilon_{\rm reg}=0$ and $T_{\rm warmup}$ iterations, measuring the residual angular error against a reference subspace; the floor estimate is then this residual error, and $\varepsilon_{\rm reg}$ is set accordingly. The heuristic, however, requires a reference subspace, which is exactly what the algorithm is trying to compute. A self-referential estimator — one that estimates the floor without a ground truth — is an open algorithmic problem. One candidate is to compare two independent runs of regularised Block MCA EXIN with different random initialisations and use their disagreement as a proxy; this is left for future implementation.

**Open question 2: optimal value of $\gamma_{\rm near}$.** Definition 17.6 fixes $\gamma_{\rm near}=0.05$ as the threshold separating the regular regime from Type-B near-degeneracy. The choice is heuristic, inherited from the Level-2 trigger of Chapter 15. An optimal value of $\gamma_{\rm near}$ would minimise the total cost of the unified gate (including both the cost of false positives, where Block MCA EXIN is invoked unnecessarily, and false negatives, where RQI is invoked on a degenerate cluster and saturates). The optimum depends on $d$, $\kappa_A$, and $\varepsilon_{\rm mach}$, but no closed-form expression is available. An empirical sweep over $\gamma_{\rm near}\in[0.01,0.20]$ on the tasks of Chapter 18 would identify a robust default, and is a natural follow-up experiment.

**Open question 3: empirical validation on real-scale training.** All experiments in this chapter are synthetic ($d\le200$, single growth event, controlled spectrum). The behaviour of the unified gate on real-scale Transformer training — where $d\in\{768,1024,2048\}$, the residual operator $A_{\rm res}$ evolves dynamically over millions of update steps, and the spectral structure interacts with optimiser-induced perturbations (Adam, weight decay, gradient clipping) — is not characterised here. The most direct test is the predicted re-run of the SARS-CoV-2 experiment of Chapter 18 under Algorithm 7, which by the prediction of §17.6.4 should terminate at $K_{\rm struct}^*\approx3$ in place of the observed $K^*=6$. This re-run requires the implementation of Algorithm 7 in PyTorch and integration into the existing INCRT training loop; it falls outside the scope of the present chapter but is the natural empirical validation gate for the framework.

**Open question 4: online tracking of drifting eigenspaces.** The classification in Definition 17.6 is made at a growth event, but in a real INCRT trajectory the residual operator $A_{\rm res}(t)$ is itself moving. The cluster size $m(t)$, the external gap $\delta_{\rm out}(t)$, and the rank

threshold classification may change during training. The moving-target results of Chapter 16 control tracking under bounded drift, but they do not yet give a complete regime-switching theory for the unified gate: a Type-B cluster may split, a regular direction may enter a near-degenerate cluster, and a small structural eigenvalue may cross the numerical rank floor. A complete theory should specify hysteresis rules for the relative gap test, dwell-time conditions for stable reclassification, and error bounds for subspace tracking when the target is an eigenspace rather than a single eigenvector. This problem is the natural numerical counterpart of the moving-target analysis of Chapter 16.

**Open question 5: optimiser-induced perturbations of the residual spectrum.** Chapter 16 studies Adam-like preconditioning and shows how the gate dynamics can be interpreted under a modified metric. The present chapter treats optimiser effects only through the aggregate operator perturbation $E$ or $\sigma_{\rm eff}$. A sharper theory should characterise how Adam, momentum, weight decay, gradient clipping, and learning-rate schedules modify the eigenvalue gaps of $A_{\rm res}$, especially near the Type-B/regular boundary and near the Type-C numerical floor. This would turn the heuristic noise term in Lemma 17.2 into an optimiser-dependent spectral perturbation bound, and would clarify when the unified gate should dispatch before or after preconditioning. The problem belongs conceptually to the interface between the Adam-extension analysis of Chapter 16 and the finite-arithmetic dispatch mechanism developed here.

<a id="section-17-8-3"></a>
### 17.8.3 What is and is not at stake

The five open questions above stratify by a useful axis: how much of the present chapter would change if the question turned out to have a negative answer.

- Open Question 1 (adaptive floor estimation) is an operational refinement: a negative answer means $\varepsilon_{\rm reg}$ must be hand-tuned per task, which is the current default in any case. No theorem is invalidated.
- Open Question 2 (optimal $\gamma_{\rm near}$) is a parameter-tuning question: a negative answer means the heuristic value 0.05 remains the default, with possibly suboptimal performance on specific tasks. The convergence theorem is unaffected.
- Open Question 3 (real-scale validation) is the most direct practical stress test of the chapter: if the unified gate performs worse than the Chapter 15 gate on real-scale tasks — because, say, the iteration floor of Lemma 17.10 dominates over realistic spectra — then the practical recommendation of this chapter would need revision. The probability of this is low: the synthetic experiments cover the relevant regimes (regular, Type-B, Type-C) and the unified gate reduces to the Chapter 15 gate in the regular case, so the worst case is parity. But the question is structurally important and remains genuinely open.
- Open Question 4 (drifting eigenspaces) is a stability question: a negative answer would not invalidate the fixed-operator convergence results, but would require additional hysteresis, smoothing, or delayed dispatch in real training loops.
- Open Question 5 (optimiser-induced perturbations) is an interface question: a negative answer to simple optimiser-invariant formulations would not invalidate the unified gate, but would force the dispatch test to be calibrated separately for each optimiser family.

The structural limitations of §17.8.1, by contrast, are not open questions: they are known caveats that constrain the scope of applicability of the chapter’s results. They have been stated explicitly so that future work can decide which of them are worth addressing first.
